# MLI-001 Research Pack

## Working title

**7 Quiet Signs Someone Is More Attracted to You Than They Let On**

## Research thesis

No single behavior proves attraction. The strongest evidence comes from **converging patterns**: repeated direct engagement, mutual eye contact, synchrony, reciprocal self-disclosure, responsiveness, and attempts to continue contact.

This episode should teach the viewer how to distinguish:
- one cue vs a pattern;
- friendliness vs selective investment;
- general personality vs behavior that changes around one person;
- attraction vs certainty.

## Evidence hierarchy

### Strongest foundation: attraction is related to enacted behavior

A 2018 meta-analysis synthesized 309 effect sizes (N = 5,422) and found a significant association between self-reported attraction and enacted behavior.

Important findings:
- direct behaviors such as **physical proximity** and **talking to someone** were more strongly related to attraction than indirect cues;
- eye contact, smiling, laughter and mimicry were also associated with attraction;
- fear of rejection can suppress direct behavior.

Implication for the script:
> Attraction can show up behaviorally, but shy or rejection-sensitive people may hide direct signals.

Source:
Montoya, Kershaw & Prosser (2018), Psychological Bulletin.
PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29733622/

## Candidate signs

### 1. They repeatedly create opportunities to be near you or talk to you

Evidence:
The attraction-behavior meta-analysis found **physical proximity** and **talking to** to be among the stronger behavioral correlates of self-reported attraction.

Why it survives:
This is more useful than vague body-language claims because it reflects behavioral investment.

Caveat:
Work, school, friendship or convenience can create proximity too. The meaningful cue is **selective and repeated proximity beyond what the situation requires**.

Source:
Montoya et al. (2018)
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29733622/

---

### 2. Eye contact becomes unusually mutual and sustained

Evidence:
A 2024 speed-dating study used dual mobile eye-tracking across 240 dates. Participants were more likely to choose partners with whom they shared more eye contact. Receiving eye contact also predicted individual mate choice.

Why it survives:
This is direct dating-context evidence, not merely folk wisdom.

Caveat:
Eye contact varies by culture, personality, anxiety and neurodiversity. It should never be treated as proof by itself.

Source:
"Sharing and Receiving Eye-Contact Predicts Mate Choice After a 5-Minute Conversation"
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10920202/

---

### 3. Your behavior starts to synchronize

Evidence:
The 2018 meta-analysis identified mimicry among behaviors associated with attraction.
A 2023 review also describes behavioral synchrony as relevant to attraction, courtship and relationship formation.

Examples:
- similar pace of speech;
- matching posture or gestures;
- conversational rhythm becoming smoother;
- laughing at the same moments.

Why it survives:
Synchrony is more defensible than simplistic claims such as "they copy your body language because they like you."

Caveat:
Synchrony can occur in friendship, rapport and cooperative interaction too.

Sources:
Montoya et al. (2018)
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29733622/

Pfaus et al. (2023)
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37544764/

---

### 4. They disclose more personal information — and invite you to do the same

Evidence:
A meta-analysis on self-disclosure and liking found:
- people disclose more to people they already like;
- people tend to like others more after disclosing to them;
- greater intimacy in disclosure is associated with greater liking.

A later review describes disclosure as central to relationship development.

Why it survives:
Self-disclosure is one of the strongest relationship-development mechanisms we found.

Caveat:
Some personalities disclose easily to everyone. The useful cue is **selective depth, reciprocity and progression over time**.

Sources:
Collins & Miller (1994)
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7809308/

Willems, Finkenauer & Kerkhof (2020)
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31430615/

---

### 5. They are unusually responsive to what you reveal

Evidence:
Research on intimacy shows that self-disclosure alone is not enough; **perceived partner responsiveness** is a major part of how intimacy develops.

A speed-dating study also found that positive affective presence and perceived responsiveness predicted romantic interest and wanting to see a date again.

Practical manifestations:
- they follow up on what you say;
- they respond emotionally rather than just factually;
- they make you feel heard;
- they build on your disclosures instead of redirecting the conversation.

Why it survives:
This moves beyond "they remember tiny details" into a better-supported construct: responsiveness.

Sources:
Laurenceau, Barrett & Pietromonaco (1998)
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9599440/

Affective presence in speed dating
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25750481/

---

### 6. The interaction becomes linguistically and behaviorally more "in sync"

Evidence:
A speed-dating study found that greater **language style matching** predicted mutual romantic interest. Pairs above the median on language style matching were substantially more likely to mutually want future contact.

Why it survives:
This is a subtle, genuinely "quiet" signal and gives the video a more original angle than generic body-language lists.

Important framing:
Do not tell viewers to consciously count function words. Explain it as:
> conversations begin to feel easier, more reciprocal and more rhythmically aligned.

Source:
Ireland et al. (2011), Psychological Science
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21149854/

---

### 7. They create a path to future interaction

Evidence:
Longitudinal speed-dating research shows that initial romantic desire predicts later direct messaging, dates and relationship initiation.
The language-style study also used mutual desire for future contact as an outcome related to conversational matching.

Practical manifestations:
- suggesting another conversation;
- mentioning an event you could both attend;
- sending a message after the interaction;
- finding a plausible reason to continue contact.

Why it survives:
This is behavior with cost and direction. It is generally more informative than passive signs.

Caveat:
Future contact can reflect friendship. Interpretation depends on the whole pattern.

Sources:
Joel et al. (2022), PNAS
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36322750/

Ireland et al. (2011)
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21149854/

---

## Signals rejected or demoted

### "They remember every detail about you"
Demoted.
Memory may reflect attention, but the research reviewed here does not justify treating detail recall as a standalone romantic-attraction signal.

### "They get nervous around you"
Demoted.
Nervousness can reflect attraction, social anxiety, intimidation, novelty or unrelated stress.

### "Their pupils dilate"
Rejected for this episode.
Too difficult for viewers to assess reliably and too vulnerable to lighting/physiology confounds.

### "Their feet point toward you"
Rejected.
Too weak and overclaimed in popular body-language content.

### "They tease you"
Demoted.
Humor dynamics can relate to attraction, but teasing itself is too ambiguous.

Related evidence:
Li et al. (2009) found that humor initiation and positive humor response varied with initial attraction.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19407005/

## Core narrative insight

The episode should build toward one key rule:

> **Do not look for one magical sign. Look for convergence.**

A useful three-part heuristic:

1. **Consistency** — does it happen repeatedly?
2. **Selectivity** — do they behave this way more with you than with everyone else?
3. **Reciprocity** — do they respond when you invest, disclose or initiate?

The seventh sign can become the synthesis:
> the strongest "sign" is not a single cue but several signals pointing in the same direction.

## Scientific caution

This is not a diagnostic system.
Attraction is private mental state and cannot be inferred with certainty from observable behavior.

Behavior is also shaped by:
- shyness;
- fear of rejection;
- culture;
- neurodiversity;
- personality;
- friendship;
- social norms;
- context.

The script should explicitly state this once near the beginning and reinforce it at the end.
