# MLI_EP003 — Why Being Helpful Is Making You Overwhelmed

Production script v1. Fictional illustrative story; no diagnosis or promise of universal outcomes.


## Block 1

### SC01 — The borrowed evening

On Monday, he gets home with one simple plan: eat something, open his sketchbook, and finally spend an evening on his own project. Then his phone lights up. Someone needs a small favor.

### SC02 — The easy yes

Nothing dramatic. A colleague wants another pair of eyes on a presentation. Ten minutes, apparently. He says yes before checking what the favor involves. Being helpful feels easier than making the conversation awkward.

### SC03 — Work arrives

The presentation needs more than a quick look. A chart makes no sense. The conclusion is missing. He fixes one thing, then another. His own sketchbook stays closed beside the laptop.

### SC04 — Dinner waits

When he finally stops, dinner is cold and the evening has gone. His colleague sends a cheerful thank you. He types a smiling reply, then looks at the project he never started.

### SC05 — Another morning

The next morning, he tells himself it was only one evening. Helping a colleague is a good thing. And it is. The problem becomes visible only when the same decision keeps happening.

### SC06 — A new request

By Tuesday, there is another request. By Wednesday, another. Each one seems reasonable on its own. Together, they begin to fill the spaces where his own plans were supposed to live.

### SC07 — Invisible cost

Nobody sees those missing hours. They see someone capable, friendly, and willing. He sees the same unfinished page every night. How did becoming dependable make his own life feel so difficult to begin?

### SC08 — Question before change

The answer is not that kindness is a mistake. It is that a quick yes can hide a much larger decision. To change the pattern, he first has to see what he is actually agreeing to.


## Block 2

### SC09 — Immediate relief

Think about the moment a request arrives. There are two possible discomforts. Saying no might disappoint someone right now. Saying yes might disappoint your future self. Only one of those people is standing in front of you.

### SC10 — The future shift

The future cost is easy to postpone. You imagine finding time later, working a little faster, or catching up at the weekend. Your future evening becomes the place where today's uncomfortable decisions disappear.

### SC11 — Borrowed confidence

Meanwhile, the person asking may have no idea how difficult refusing feels. Research on requests for help suggests that askers can underestimate this social pressure. A casual request can feel surprisingly heavy to receive.

### SC12 — The reasonable asker

That does not mean every person asking is selfish or manipulative. They may genuinely think you have room. If you keep saying yes without explaining the tradeoff, they have little reason to know otherwise.

### SC13 — The missing information

From their side, the arrangement seems to work. They ask, you agree, the task gets done. Your lost evening, delayed project, and growing frustration are missing information. Politeness has made the cost invisible.

### SC14 — Private resentment

Eventually, he starts resenting messages from people he actually likes. A familiar name appears and his shoulders tighten. He is angry about commitments they heard him accept, even though he never wanted to make them.

### SC15 — A different explanation

This is where the story can go wrong. He could decide that everyone takes advantage of kindness. Or he could notice something more useful: his answer is not giving people an accurate picture of his capacity.

### SC16 — Useful distinction

Being kind and being constantly available are different choices. You can care about someone's problem without becoming the person who solves all of it. That distinction sounds obvious, until someone you like asks for help.


## Block 3

### SC17 — Saturday promise

On Saturday, he finally sits down to draw. There is no emergency and no meeting. Just an hour he has promised himself. Then a friend asks whether he can help move a few boxes.

### SC18 — The awkward blank

He hesitates. He cannot say he is working. He is not traveling. There is nothing official on the calendar. Compared with someone else's practical problem, his own quiet afternoon suddenly feels difficult to defend.

### SC19 — Real space

But free time is not automatically spare time. Rest, friendship, and a personal project do not need an audience to matter. An empty calendar can still contain something you deliberately chose to protect.

### SC20 — The boxes

He goes anyway. The move takes longer than expected. His friend is grateful and buys lunch. Nothing terrible happens. That is what makes the pattern hard to question: each individual favor looks perfectly harmless.

### SC21 — The cost after

The cost appears afterward, when the good light has gone and his energy has gone with it. The drawing is still there. He tells himself he can do it tomorrow, using the same promise as before.

### SC22 — Repeated choice

One postponed afternoon does not ruin a life. But repeated choices create a direction. If your own plans always lose whenever somebody asks, eventually they become plans you no longer expect yourself to keep.

### SC23 — No scorekeeping

The point is not to keep a secret score of every favor. Helping can be meaningful and enjoyable. The question is whether this particular yes fits the time, energy, and commitments you actually have.

### SC24 — A better question

Instead of asking, would a good person help, he tries a more precise question: what will I have to move if I agree? Suddenly, the decision has a shape. Something real must make room.


## Block 4

### SC25 — The next request

The next request arrives on Monday. His colleague needs help with another presentation. This time, he does not answer immediately. He says, let me check what I have on, and I will get back to you.

### SC26 — Space to decide

That sentence does not reject anyone. It creates a small gap between being asked and becoming committed. In that gap, he can consider the task without also managing the other person's expression in real time.

### SC27 — Define the job

Then he asks what help actually means. Is it a quick opinion on the opening, a complete rewrite, or staying late to finish the slides? Those are different requests, even if all three begin with a favor.

### SC28 — Find the edge

He can offer twenty minutes to review the structure. He cannot take ownership of the whole presentation. A specific offer gives both people an edge they can recognize, instead of an agreement that keeps expanding.

### SC29 — A useful limit

He tells her he can look at the opening tomorrow, but he cannot stay late tonight. It is a smaller yes than she hoped for. It is also an answer he can actually keep without sacrificing his evening.

### SC30 — The ordinary response

She pauses, then says that works. The moment passes. There is no dramatic confrontation. He notices how much preparation he had done for a disaster that, on this occasion, never arrived. Next time might feel different.

### SC31 — When no is needed

Some requests still need a no. He does not have to invent an appointment or produce a convincing excuse. He can say he cannot help this weekend. A truthful answer can be brief without being cruel.

### SC32 — No courtroom

He also stops treating every explanation like a courtroom defense. More detail is sometimes useful, especially with people who depend on him. But a boundary does not become valid only after nobody can object to it.


## Block 5

### SC33 — The difficult silence

The surprising part comes after he sends the message. He still feels uncomfortable. His mind starts offering amendments: perhaps he could manage it, perhaps he sounded unfriendly, perhaps he should explain a little more.

### SC34 — Let the moment pass

He waits before sending another message. Discomfort is information about how the moment feels; it is not, by itself, proof that he chose badly. He can review his decision without immediately undoing it.

### SC35 — A disappointed friend

Sometimes a friend is disappointed. That possibility is real. A relationship can include a request, a refusal, and a little disappointment without either person becoming the villain. The next step is a conversation, not a diagnosis.

### SC36 — The power difference

Work can be more complicated. A manager controls priorities, and simply refusing may carry consequences. So he asks which task should move if this new one comes first. The conversation becomes about workload and choices.

### SC37 — Visible priorities

He explains that finishing the presentation today means delaying the other assignment. Which should take priority? This does not guarantee a reasonable response. It does make the tradeoff visible, instead of silently absorbing more work.

### SC38 — Real obligations

And some obligations deserve the inconvenience. A friend facing a genuine problem may need more than a tidy time slot. He wants room for that kind of help. Protecting capacity can make those choices easier to honor.

### SC39 — A chosen yes

This time, he chooses to help and adjusts his own plans honestly. He is not pretending the favor costs nothing. Because the decision is deliberate, gratitude feels welcome instead of becoming payment for resentment.

### SC40 — Flexible not automatic

There is no perfect rule that works for every request. The aim is a more accurate answer: yes when he can mean it, no when he cannot, and a clear discussion when responsibility or circumstances make it complicated.


## Block 6

### SC41 — The protected hour

A week later, he sits down with his sketchbook again. His phone is nearby, but the hour has already been chosen. If a nonurgent request arrives, he can answer after he has done what he came to do.

### SC42 — Still imperfect

He does not become instantly confident. He still catches himself agreeing too quickly. Sometimes he has to return to a conversation and clarify what he can offer. Changing a familiar response takes practice, not a new personality.

### SC43 — Repair a promise

When he has already promised too much, he raises it early. He explains what changed and offers a realistic alternative where possible. The point is to become more reliable about commitments, including the ones he makes to himself.

### SC44 — Time becomes visible

Over time, the difference is ordinary rather than spectacular. A drawing gets finished. Dinner is eaten while it is warm. He meets a friend without privately calculating all the work waiting for him afterward.

### SC45 — Present company

He still helps people. He still cares whether they are struggling. But his kindness no longer depends on pretending that his time has no value. Being present feels different when he has chosen to be there.

### SC46 — One experiment

Try it with one request this week. Before answering, ask what the task involves and what would have to move. If you need a moment, take it. Then give an answer that matches what you can actually do.

### SC47 — The finished page

You do not have to make yourself difficult to reach in order to matter. You do not need to teach everyone a lesson. You need a way to be generous that leaves room for a life of your own.

### SC48 — An honest answer

The goal is not to say no more often just for the sake of it. It is to make your yes honest. When your own promises count too, helping someone else becomes a choice you can stand behind.
